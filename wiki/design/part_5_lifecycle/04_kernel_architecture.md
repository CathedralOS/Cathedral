# Chapter 04: Kernel Architecture

> The privileged core is the smallest trusted substrate that can carry Cathedral's component, capability, and update story.

## The Legacy Model

Monolithic kernels fuse everything privileged (drivers, filesystems, network stacks, schedulers) into one address space where any bug is a total compromise. Microkernels split that out into servers but pay in IPC overhead and complexity, and in practice still grow a fat trusted base. Hybrids inherit both sets of problems. Across all of them the privileged boundary is drawn by hardware rings and address spaces, and the question "what must actually be trusted, and what can crash or be replaced without killing the system?" is answered by accident of history rather than by design.

## The Cathedral Model

The privileged core is the smallest substrate that supports the component, capability, and update story, not the cleanest-sounding diagram. The architecture is not chosen by aesthetics ("microkernel sounds clean"). It is chosen by asking, for each piece of the system: what must be trusted, what can be restarted, what can be upgraded, what can be proven, and what can crash without killing the system? The answer follows from Omega's isolation guarantees, not from x86 ring boundaries.

### The wall sits between OS code and app code

The boundary is OS code versus app code. The OS is written in Omega and is proof-carrying, so its own components isolate from one another by the type system rather than the MMU. That is what makes a Theseus-style single address space and frictionless hot swap ([[updates_and_hot_swap]]) possible for the OS. App code is the other side. A user app can be written in anything, C++ included, so the OS cannot trust its instruction stream. It isolates the app with hardware (separate address space, MMU, instruction trapping) while the capability model confines its system access. Language isolation is the OS's internal mechanism. Hardware isolation is the wall between the OS and untrusted apps. That is the normal case, not just legacy boxes, and where the wall sits is the architectural decision.

### The core is trusted, not contained

The core itself is the one thing on neither side of that wall. It is trusted, not contained, the exact inverse of a driver, which is contained, not trusted. You cannot cage the core, because the core is what builds the cages. It programs the IOMMU, the MMIO maps, and the device resets, so there is nothing above it to confine it. Its assurance comes from proof and TCB minimization, not a wall. The whole system therefore reduces to a dichotomy. Every part is either **caged** (untrusted and hardware-confined: apps, drivers) or **proved** (the trusted core, kept as small as possible), with only a thin, hand-audited hardware seam left trusted and unproven. This also fixes the test for what can ever be required of a component: only what the cage, meaning hardware plus core, enforces over the component's head. Anything asked of an untrusted component is best-effort, safe to rely on only with a hardware backstop.

## Concerns & Design Space

- **The important axis.** Trust, restartability, upgradability, provability, and crash isolation are evaluated per subsystem (drivers, FS, net, scheduler) rather than as one global verdict.
- **Single address space plus language isolation.** Zero-copy IPC and frictionless hot swap, conditioned on proved Omega. The boundary-provider set becomes much of the TCB ([[omega_substrate]]).
- **SAS safety is proof or a capability-ISA.** The cheap call and the proof dependence are one coin.
  - A single address space buys near-zero "syscalls". A call into an OS service is a plain function call: no trap, no ring switch, no TLB flush. Singularity measured roughly 100× over a traditional syscall. The gain exists because there is no MMU boundary to cross.
  - That is exactly why memory safety inside the SAS must come from elsewhere: either PCC validating pointer accesses (software proof) or a CHERI-style capability-ISA, where unforgeable hardware bounds are checked by the CPU on every dereference. CHERI is real; ARM Morello shipped in 2022.
  - CHERI gives both, shared-address-space cheap calls and hardware-enforced isolation with no PCC, so a capability-ISA turns the whole performance story from a bet into a hardware guarantee. On commodity hardware without it, the SAS rests on the proof.
  - The proof dependence is localized. App-to-app and app-to-OS isolation is the MMU (apps are hardware-walled, below), so a checker or PCC failure can never leak one app's memory to another. It could only dent the core's internal isolation.
  - The proof is therefore a performance bet over an MMU-safe baseline, scoped to the OS core. If it fails, cross-core-component calls fall back to IPC cost, a slower capability microkernel, never a safety break.
- **The case against SAS-via-PCC, and the lean toward MMU then CHERI.** The SAS-on-software-proof bet keeps adding costs as chapters mature, and CHERI is the more grounded direction.
  - The costs: recompile-and-hot-swap for in-place changes, proof-aware foreign boundaries, and debugging. Raw byte-patching (`int3`, trampolines) cannot be injected into the SAS without breaking its sole invariant, that only checked code is admitted, so SAS-native instrumentation must be checker-verified or the component temporarily walled ([[debugging_and_tracing]]).
  - Scheduler fairness is not one of those proof costs. Cathedral's timer may architecturally preempt native code at any instruction. Only structured cancellation and migration latency depend on declared semantic safe points ([[scheduler_and_resources]]).
  - PCC for a serious systems language has been done essentially zero times in history. Necula-style PCC proved narrow type safety on small programs. CompCert and seL4 are whole-program expert verification, not modular proof that travels with arbitrary shipped code. SAS-via-PCC bets on inventing the unprecedented.
  - CHERI gives memory safety and compartment crossings cheaper than the MMU, in hardware, with no PCC. Morello shipped in 2022 and CHERIoT covers embedded. It is not free: a compartment switch costs more than the ideal SAS's plain call, and it bets on capability silicon reaching the target platform. But that is a hardware-availability bet on something real, not a research bet on something unprecedented.
  - The lean: MMU-by-default plus CHERI-where-available is the plausible shipping architecture, and SAS-via-PCC is the aspirational ceiling. The ceiling is kept because the MMU fallback is always correct, which is the point of the localized-bet framing, not because the proof is expected to land first.
- **Shrinking the compiler in the TCB.** Because SAS isolation rests on proof, the compiler is in the TCB. A miscompile breaks every boundary at once, with no MMU backstop. The answer is the same shrink-and-bottom-out move used everywhere else: a hand-audited bootstrap seed, so no Thompson trusting-trust binary can hide in the lineage; a small proof-checking kernel that re-checks compiled artifacts; and a verified Omega-to-machine translation that the kernel checks, CompCert or CakeML style. Trust then bottoms out at a tiny, legible **{seed, checker, specs, hardware}**. It is never zero, since a system cannot prove its own foundation (Gödel) and a proof only shows code meets its spec, never that the spec is the right one. But it is small enough to read by hand. This becomes canon once Omega self-hosts. Until then the Rust-implemented compiler leans on the differential oracle as the interim mitigation ([Omega: Cathedral alignment](https://github.com/CathedralOS/Omega/blob/main/wiki/drafts/reference/cathedral_alignment.md)).
- **One first-party checker.** Of the `{seed, checker, specs, hardware}` base, the checker is Omega's own: the bootstrap lattice's proof checker, with no third-party tooling. It is the single trust anchor that validates everything by re-checking: compiled-code certs (Thompson-resistance), verification certs (inductive proofs), and library proofs (a native `Proofs.lock`-style construct, Cargo-to-Rust). Auditing that one tiny verified-to-machine-code checker is the irreducible human cost, and it is accepted. `cake_lpr`, a SAT checker verified to machine code, is the existence proof that this is practical.
- **The spec is the soft underbelly.** A proof shows code meets spec, never that the spec is the right spec, and that gap is unsolvable because intent is not formalizable. The discipline is to keep specs small, legible, and tested, so that human review, the only thing that touches the gap, gets its best shot, and to never sell "proven" as "correct". Certification reads "proven against spec S", not "correct". This is empirically where failures cluster: about 96% of real zero-knowledge soundness bugs are spec or encoding errors, not proof-checking errors.
- **Proof is a gradient.** Verification is not all-or-nothing. Every component rides the free floor: memory safety always, bounds and totality cheaply. Deep functional proofs are an opt-in tier reserved for load-bearing code. "Designed to be verified" therefore means it can be, and the critical parts are, not that everything is proven. Safety is proven unconditionally. Liveness rides the trusted scheduler's fairness ([[scheduler_and_resources]]). The real cost is proof authoring, not checking, which is the LLM-authoring bet in Omega's `totality_and_bounded_computation.md`.
- **Side channels are parity, not a SAS penalty.** SAS does not worsen microarchitectural leakage. The MMU was never a side-channel defense (Spectre and Meltdown cross it anyway). SAS drops it only between mutually-trusting core components, and untrusted code stays hardware-walled and unmapped from the core, so exposure is about that of any OS, arguably better on the Meltdown vector. Proof gives no edge here, since proof is architectural and side channels are microarchitectural. The default is baseline hardware mitigations everywhere plus constant-time crypto. Constant-time code rests on one hardware fact, the CPU's data-independent-timing guarantee (ARM DIT, Intel DOITM), which sits in the hardware entry of the trust base.
- **Isolation classes for hardened domains.** Beyond the default, a domain carries a declared **`IsolationClass`**, a total-order ladder `baseline → flush → partition → exclusive`. It is an OS-defined `data` value, set on the component or raised per span by an ownership-scoped guard, gated at spawn by capability and metered. The core enforces it ([[scheduler_and_resources]]). Temporal leaks are handled by a flush at the context switch, keyed to the pair: exit-flush the outgoing hardened domain's cache, and entry-flush predictors before an incoming one. Spatial leaks are handled by placement. The unit is the physical core, whose SMT threads share L1 and predictors: `exclusive` owns the core, and `partition` co-locates only trusted work and partitions L3. Require equals cage: the core flushes and places, and the component only declares.
- **Hardware isolation for app code and for drivers.** Any code the Omega toolchain did not compile and prove, which is most user apps in any language, is untrusted at the instruction level. It is isolated by MMU and address space and by instruction trapping, with capabilities confining its system access ([[security_policy_and_sandboxing]]). Drivers are the same case by design. They run as confined user-mode components, contained not trusted, so a bad driver corrupts only its own device ([[driver_model]]). Because device DMA bypasses the CPU's MMU, containing a device needs an **IOMMU**, and Cathedral mandates one as the hardware floor.
- **An LLM-era policy option: only checkable code runs.** A stricter policy refuses foreign binaries entirely. That makes the hardware wall optional defense-in-depth rather than a load-bearing foreign-code mechanism, and dissolves the compat-box and SFI complexity. It is viable because LLMs make producing a compliant binary cheap, and Omega gains adoption before any compliant foreign-language compiler ships. The only-checkable-runs constraint becomes a coherence and security feature, at the cost of a smaller initial ecosystem.
- **What stays privileged.** The minimal substrate likely owns only: capability enforcement; extent/address-space management including IOMMU mappings and MMIO-view minting; installation of immutable admitted executable artifacts; scheduling primitives; interrupt-entry installation and the external-root ledger; provider admission/selection; and the hardware root-of-trust handoff. There is no general executable-memory/JIT authority. Checked assembly implements the hardware seam, while plan/capability contracts keep each operation attributable ([[hardware_foundation_profile]], [[driver_model]], [[boot_and_trust_chain]]).
- **Code injection and control-flow integrity are separate concerns.** The admitted-artifact loader prevents ordinary bytes from becoming host code. Checked-Omega backward-edge returns derive from memory safety and compiler-owned, non-addressable live stack/control state; WCSU supplies fixed stack-capacity evidence. Forward-edge indirect calls and tail calls still require sealed requirement-compatible entry references or descriptors, while admitted opaque providers must preserve their call/state exits or remain isolated. Independent final-byte transfer validation and CET/PAC/shadow-stack hardening are later PCC/TCB assurance, not an Omega source-language or timer prerequisite ([[hardware_foundation_profile]]).
- **Restartable subsystems.** Everything not in the minimal core should be a restartable, upgradable component, including most of what monolithic kernels keep privileged.
- **Minimal trusted broker: route tickets, never hold authority.** Unavoidable middlemen (network demux, observability and audit query, service-activator routing) stay TCB-worthy by routing capability tickets they cannot themselves redeem. A `{slot, generation}` ticket only redeems in the target's arena, so the broker holds no authority and revocation stays the target's generation bump. The discipline per broker:
  - Name the one irreducible trusted invariant and prove only that. For demux: this flow reaches the right inbox, never cross-delivered.
  - Keep classification and routing trusted, but push processing to untrusted endpoints. The whole TCP stack lives in each world.
  - Inspect headers, never payload. Seal payloads so the broker sits structurally outside the confidentiality TCB.
  - Never let trusted routing depend on the untrusted driver.
  - Lean on hardware steering (per-world NIC queues, SR-IOV) so the software broker shrinks toward zero.

  The smell test: a broker that looks up "am I allowed?" holds power, which is wrong. One that is handed what to route does not. If a broker needs a standing capability, a primitive is missing from the core, so add the primitive rather than fatten the broker.
- **Options on the table.** Monolithic, microkernel, hybrid, exokernel-ish, library-OS-ish, Theseus-style SAS, capability kernel, hypervisor-first. These are held as candidates, not commitments, until the target hardware and workload constrain them.

## Key Questions

- What is the irreducible privileged core, the set whose compromise is fatal, and how small can it be made?
- Where is the line drawn between language-isolated OS code (proved Omega, single address space) and hardware-isolated app code (untrusted, any language)?
- How much of the classic kernel can become restartable components without losing the performance the target workload needs?
- Is a hypervisor-first base worth it for running a legacy box ([[compatibility_and_legacy]]) safely alongside the native system?

## Omega Leverage

- **Language-level isolation** is the substrate's premise: ownership, borrowing, and effect/authority checking provide isolation that the MMU otherwise provides, per [Omega Chapter 19: Capabilities, Reach, And Boundaries](https://github.com/CathedralOS/Omega/blob/main/wiki/language_guide/chapter_19_capabilities_effects_boundaries.md).
- **The boundary-provider set is much of the TCB.** Omega's boundary registry makes the trusted base enumerable. The registered providers are what must be trusted, and the build report lists them.
- **`reaches` + authority-flow ceilings** let even privileged components be bounded and audited, so "privileged" is not synonymous with "unbounded."
- **The hardware foundation** separates inert addresses, extent authority, layout/access plans, checked instructions, entry roots, and external loans, so low-level code does not regain C's "number equals power" escape ([[hardware_foundation_profile]]).
- **Replaceable provider realizations** ([Omega Chapter 14: Versioned Data](https://github.com/CathedralOS/Omega/blob/main/wiki/language_guide/chapter_14_traits.md#versioned-data)) make core subsystems upgradable where their owned closure, resource provision, entry accounting, and state-disposition policy can be validated. A package or arbitrary machine is not a swap point merely by existing.

## Open Questions

- How small can the irreducible {seed, checker, specs, hardware} base inside the proved core be made, and who audits it? Miscompilation is answered by the TCB-minimization strategy above and side channels by the `IsolationClass` posture (parity plus opt-in flush and placement), so this human-audited, never-zero base is what remains. Drivers sit outside it, being hardware-confined rather than language-trusted ([[driver_model]]).
- Where is the empirical performance crossover? The SAS win is real inside the proved core (IPC-heavy, service-mesh, and OS-service workloads, possibly by multiples), Linux-class at the untrusted-app seam, and parity or slower for compute-bound work until the optimizer matures, which Omega's higher ceiling plus its verified-LLM-optimization loop (the verified gated ML optimizer brief) should accelerate. The open part is how much of a real desktop workload is seam-dominated.
- Does the architecture decision even resolve before the target platform is fixed, or is it deferred until then ([[vision_and_non_goals]])?
